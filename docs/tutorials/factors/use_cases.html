When to Use Factors

You now know how to create factors and how they differ from atomic vectors. This page asks a different question. How do you decide when to use a factor in your own data analysis projects?

Good Candidates for Factors

At this point, it should come as no surprise when I tell you that R factors are meant to represent nominal variables. In other words, we use factors to represent unordered groups like the following.

  • Political party affiliation: {Democrat, Republican}
  • Nationality: {Dutch, Canadian, Japanese, Kenyan, Chilean}
  • Biological classification: {Mammal, Bird, Fish, Insect}

Factors are also appropriate representations of qualitative labels or the answer to questions with fixed response sets.

  • A student’s exam success: {Pass, Fail}
  • A politician’s vote for a bill: {Support, Oppose, Abstain}
  • The outcome from scoring an exam question: {Correct, Incorrect}

What about Ordered Categories?

Basic R factors are not appropriate for ordinal variables. That is, qualitative variables with meaningful ordering like the following.

  • Responses to questionnaire items given on a Likert-type scale: {Strongly Disagree, Somewhat Disagree, Somewhat Agree, Strongly Agree}
  • Rankings (e.g., results of a running race): {First, Second, Third}

For this kind of ordinal data, we can use an ordered factor. To create an ordered factor, you simply specify ordered = TRUE in the factor() function.

medals <- factor(
    sample(c("bronze", "silver", "gold"), 10, TRUE),
    levels = c("bronze", "silver", "gold"),
    ordered = TRUE
)
medals
 [1] silver gold   silver silver gold   silver bronze silver gold   silver
Levels: bronze < silver < gold
attributes(medals)
$levels
[1] "bronze" "silver" "gold"  

$class
[1] "ordered" "factor"

Poor Candidates for Factors

Factors are great, but you must also be careful not to overgeneralize your use of factors. Of course, factors are never appropriate for numeric data that represent continuous quantities. However, a factor is not necessarily the right way to represent all variables that happen to take a small number of distinct values. The following examples illustrate common cases where a factor would be the wrong choice.

Low Resolution Measurements

When deciding whether or not to use a factor, you need to consider the measurement level of the hypothetical construct you’re trying to represent, not how the values look in your data. For example, your data may contain the results of an IQ test scored on a 10-point scale. Although this item can only take 10 distinct values in your dataset, you probably wouldn’t want to represent IQ with a factor. Presumably, the 10-point IQ score is meant to quantify continuously varying intelligence level. So, you’re working with a low-resolution assessment of the underlying continuous variable, not a 10-level categorical variable.

Unit Identifiers

Most datasets contain identifier columns to uniquely label observational units (e.g., participant ID, school code, timestamp). These indicator variables certainly represent some sense of “grouping” but not usually the kinds of groups that you want to include as part of you substantive analysis. So, representing unit identifies as simple character vectors or integer vectors is usually more appropriate than using a factor.

Descriptive Strings

In addition to various flavors of unit identifiers, most real-world datasets contain metadata fields like the participants’ IP addresses, software version tags, or administrative information about research sites. Likewise, many datasets contain at least some semi-structured textual data represented as character vectors (e.g., responses to open-ended questions, text scraped from websites). It almost never makes sense to directly convert these types of descriptive data into factors. There may be substantively interesting grouping information hiding in such columns, but you have to manually code the raw data into some form of systematic grouping variable before you’re ready to create any factors.

Gray Areas

The choice of whether or not to use a factor is not always an obvious, black-and-white decision. Many cases, such as the following examples, fall into situationally dependent gray areas. In these kinds of situations, the focus of our analysis typically tells us whether we should use a factor or not.

Manually Coarsened Variables

Sometimes, numeric data can be “coarsened” to create qualitative groups. We’ve already used several such cases in the examples above. Typically, to “pass” an exam, the examinee’s score must exceed some threshold. In Dutch universities, that threshold would usually be a score of 5.5 on a 1-to-10 scale. So, the binary Pass/Fail factor noted above would typically be created by binning a continuous, numeric score.

\[ \begin{align} \mathit{Success} = \begin{cases} \mathit{Fail} & \text{if} & \mathit{Score} < 5.5\\ \mathit{Pass} & \text{if} & \mathit{Score} \geq 5.5 \end{cases} \end{align} \]

This type of manual coarsening is often used in biomedical contexts to simplify interpretation. Anytime medical guidance is presented in terms of a small set of disjoint groups, there’s a good chance that the groups were defined by categorizing an underlying continuous variable. BMI categories are one commonly encountered example. Technically, BMI can take any positive value, but many public health organizations provide guidelines for “healthy” and “unhealthy” BMIs based on discrete ranges. For example, the World Health Organization defines six levels of Nutritional Status based on the following BMI ranges.

\[ \begin{align} \mathit{Nutritional~Status} = \begin{cases} \mathit{Underweight} & \text{if} & & & \hspace{-14mu} \mathit{BMI} < 18.5\\ \mathit{Healthy~Weight} & \text{if} & 18.5 \hspace{-18mu} & \geq & \hspace{-14mu} \mathit{BMI} < 25\\ \mathit{Overweight} & \text{if} & 25 & \geq & \hspace{-14mu} \mathit{BMI} < 30\\ \mathit{Obesity~Class~I} & \text{if} & 30 & \geq & \hspace{-14mu} \mathit{BMI} < 35\\ \mathit{Obesity~Class~II} & \text{if} & 35 & \geq & \hspace{-14mu} \mathit{BMI} < 40\\ \mathit{Obesity~Class~III} & \text{if} & 40 & \geq & \hspace{-14mu} \mathit{BMI} \end{cases} \end{align} \]

Counts

A count variable records the number of discrete elements in a set—like the number of children in a family or the number of hurricanes in a year. Technically, counts are categorical variables because you can only count whole numbers of things (e.g., a family cannot include 2.75 children). However, we often want to analyze counts as numeric values. For example, it seems sensible to ask if the average number of children in Dutch families is different from the average number of children in Spanish families. In such cases, it would not be appropriate to convert the numeric count variable to a factor.

We don’t always need to analyze counts as numeric quantities, though. Sometimes, it makes more sense to represent a count as an ordered factor. For example, a questionnaire item may ask participants to report how many alcoholic drinks they consume in a typical week or how many cigarettes they smoke each day. As posed, these questions illicit count responses, but the data are often binned into discrete ordinal categories like none, a few per week, a few per day, more than ten per day, etc. Self-reported consumption rates are notoriously poor, so analyzing these questionnaire responses as numeric counts may lead to spurious precision and misleading results. We may be better off accepting the inherent noise of our measurement and purposefully coarsening the counts into appropriately low-resolution ordered factors.

Ordinal Variables

Finally, let’s consider a problem that’s somewhat complementary to the situation with counts. Ordinal variables are unambiguously categorical, and ordered factors are the correct way to represent an ordinal variable in R. So, it may surprise you to hear that ordinal variables are analyzed as numeric vectors in most real-world analyses. Again, the main consideration is how the variables will be analyzed, not how they can be most accurately represented in the dataset.

Practice

Consider the following four hypothetical variables.

  1. A column recording each survey respondent’s marital status: {single, married, divorced, widowed}.
  2. A column recording each respondent’s exact age in years.
  3. A column recording which of 3 experimental treatment groups each participant was assigned to.
  4. A column recording each respondent’s annual income in dollars.
  •  Question
  •  Solution

Explain why (or why not) a factor would be an appropriate way to represent each variable.

  1. Factor. Marital status is a small set of unordered categories, a textbook use-case for a factor.
  2. Not a factor. Age is a genuine quantity, you’d want to compute averages, compare magnitudes, and so on.
  3. Factor. Treatment group assignment is exactly the kind of categorical grouping factors were designed for, especially since this variable will likely be used in a statistical model later.
  4. Not a factor. Income is a continuous quantity, even though it might have a skewed distribution, you wouldn’t want R to prevent you from computing a mean or a sum.

Much Ado about Nothing?

You may be wondering why we’re making such a fuss about when you should and should not use factors. Well, in practice, getting this decision right isn’t just a trivial matter of style. As you’ve seen on previous pages, if you try to analyze a nominal variable as a numeric vector, R will assume that variable represents some continuous value (implying, incorrectly, that “group 2” is somehow “twice as much” as “group 1”). In the gray area situations discussed above, the choice of whether or not to use a factor directly affects the formulation of your research question and the meaning of your results.

Beyond these technical and pragmatic considerations, there’s also a deeper level of implications. Statistical measurement is not nearly as anodyne and objective as we may wish it to be, particularly if your analysis involves human subjects or sociopolitical entities. Something as simple as the way we choose to operationalize some variable in our analysis can make very strong political statements.

For example, binary gender was, traditionally, the go-to example of a nominal variable used in statistic education. However, in recent years, the conceptualization of gender has shifted toward understanding gender as a fluid, culturally created construct. If you want to include a “gender” variable in your analysis, what operationalization will you use? If you don’t want to endorse the concept of binary gender, you could create a three-level factor by adding a “non-binary” option, but you could also fully embrace gender fluidity by representing gender as a continuous numeric variable. Each of these options makes an implicit statement about the definition of “gender”, and you must own that statement.

Lest any sociologists or political scientists reading this start getting too smug, realize that you don’t need to analyze person-level data to encounter fraught design decisions. Suppose you need to create a “nationality” indicator for a sample of East Asian participants. Do you label participants from Taiwan as Chinese? What if you need to create a “country” indicator for a sample from the Middle East. Do you represent Palestine as a country? At time of writing, both of these questions are highly contentious political issues, so you cannot simply defer to the consensus of the international community: you must choose, and your choice will have consequences.
